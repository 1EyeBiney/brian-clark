Why this page exists

When I first learned HTML, building this site would have taken me more than a month. Just before I lost my sight, it would have taken a couple of weeks. A year ago, blind, it would have taken about a week. In none of those cases would I have bothered. It was too much work for a resume. Even two months ago I would not have tried.

In the first week of November 2026 it took about four hours, and I did it without seeing a single word of it. I have been totally blind since 2015.

Two things made that possible. The newest AI models can now do this kind of work when someone directs them well. And I can direct them, check what they produce, and correct it, because I am good with a screen reader. The first of those is available to everyone. The second is what I teach.

Two organizations asked me for a resume in the same week. A resume lists where I have been. It does not show what I can do, and what I can do is the only question that matters here. So this page is the resume, with the evidence attached.

Every item below that says "live" is a link you can open and try. Every piece of software was built by a totally blind person who cannot see a screen, working through a screen reader and two AI desktop applications. If the work holds up, that is the credential.

If you have five minutes and not twenty, go straight to Things I built that you can try. It lists specific features, the problem each one solves for a blind or low-vision person, and how to try it.

How I work now

I use NVDA as my screen reader all day, every day. It is not a tool I pick up to teach a lesson and put down. It is the only way I touch a computer. When I get stuck there is no one to turn to, so I get unstuck. More than eleven years of that is where my instincts come from.

In 2026 my daily setup is NVDA plus two AI desktop applications running side by side: Claude Desktop and ChatGPT Desktop. Both are HTML-based, so NVDA reads them well. I give one a task, switch to the other, give it a task, and move between them checking work. On a good day I run two streams of work at once, something I could not do when I had sight.

What changed. A year ago a badly built website was a wall. Now I send an agent through it. A year ago I asked a sighted person what something looked like. Now I ask the AI, and I get a description that is neutral instead of filtered through someone else's assumptions. A year ago I could manage one project at a time. This year I shipped nine.

The barriers that AI removes are not abstract to me. They are the specific things that used to end my workday: an unlabeled button, a PDF that is a picture, a form that only works with a mouse, a spreadsheet I needed restructured. Each one is now a sentence I type instead of a wall I hit.

AI in my teaching work, and where client information goes

When I have students, AI is part of the ordinary teaching week. The rule I work under is that a class recording and its transcript, which hold a client's voice and everything they said, stay on my own laptop. They are processed there by AI models that run on that laptop and send nothing out.

  • After each class, the recording is transcribed on my laptop. A local model then reads the transcript the way a student would and writes class notes: what was taught, which keystrokes were used, and what to practice. It is instructed to leave out small talk and personal conversation. Those notes become the student's study-guide email. The recording and the transcript are not sent to any outside AI service.
  • Reports to Vocational Rehabilitation counselors start from those class notes, which are a record of the lessons, not of the person. Before any outside AI model sees them, the pipeline makes a separate copy with the student's name, any other names I have listed, email addresses, phone numbers, and street addresses removed, and gives me a short list of any unfamiliar names to check by hand. Only that copy goes to one of the large AI models, which helps me draft the report: what was covered, in what order, and how far the student has come against the plan. I add the client's full name and the counselor's name by hand after the draft is written, then read the whole report through before it goes out. The judgment about a student's progress is mine.
  • Outside AI services otherwise do the work that has no client in it: structuring curriculum from my own teaching notes, drafting lesson materials and quizzes, and building the tools and templates themselves.

The effort is in the scaffolding. Building a pipeline takes days of specifying and testing. Once it is built it runs with almost no extra work from me, class after class, which is how one instructor can give every student written notes after every lesson.

How I got here

I date my AI journey from the fall of 2024, and it did not start with code. It started with Reaper, the audio editor, which I decided to teach myself with ChatGPT as the instructor. I was writing comedy lyrics and feeding them to Suno, the AI music generator, and I wanted to cut the results into something I could share. Through 2025 I made a VoiceOver tutorial podcast and a couple of comedy episodes for my family, especially for my stepfather, Marty Zeis, in his final months. None of it was work. All of it mattered, because AI made me a reasonable audio engineer by answering the questions I could never have found answers to on my own. Audio editing is a visual trade, built around waveforms on a screen, and it had been out of my reach. That was the lesson: with an AI as a patient instructor, subjects that had been closed to me were open.

Around January or February of 2026 I discovered what people call vibe coding. I used the ChatGPT web interface to write JavaScript web pages for me. JavaScript is notoriously hard for a blind programmer. It is dense with symbols, and a screen reader reading a line of it is slow and unforgiving of a single wrong character. What I found is that I did not need to write it. I needed to specify it, test it, and judge whether it was usable and accessible. The AI wrote in a language I cannot read fluently, and I did the part that only a blind user can do. My first saved experiment is dated March 24, 2026.

As the projects grew, I ran into how AI actually works: context windows, memory limits, the cost of a long conversation. That pushed me to Visual Studio Code, which has a real accessibility mode, and to working with AI inside the editor on local files instead of pasting code back and forth in a browser. That is where I learned how software is built: project structure, build plans, design documents, the way a game studio breaks a large thing into pieces. The AI taught me those practices because it knows them, and then it followed them with me.

Much of what I had to manage by hand in March and April of 2026 is no longer a concern. The tools now hold memory across sessions, take actions on my behalf, operate other programs, and run several tasks at once while I review. The barriers keep falling, and the pace is not slowing. Anyone teaching technology to blind people has to be living inside this change, not reading about it.

Before AI: the reference workbook
Keyboard commands and gestures for the programs my students use are scattered over dozens of websites and are a pain to find with a screen reader. So I gathered them into one Excel workbook and kept it current for my students by hand for years: 30 sheets and roughly 1,500 entries covering JAWS, NVDA, ZoomText, Word, Excel, Outlook, web browsing, iPhone and iPad, Android TalkBack, Chromebook, and Alexa, with a change log that runs to about 80 revisions. That, and the Bosma teaching plan, are what I could produce before AI multiplied what one person can do.
Teaching philosophy, in one paragraph
The student does the task. I do not take the phone and connect it to Wi-Fi for them. Practice is the only thing that builds a confident non-visual computer user, and the biggest problem with residential training was that nobody practices at the end of a long day in a hotel with bad Wi-Fi. So the program I built is done from the student's own home, on their own time, around supported independent work. The goal is a person who can walk into an ordinary office with the skills already in hand, and who is not afraid to try things without me.

What I built for First Aid for the Blind

Client referrals slowed in 2026 as state vocational rehabilitation budgets tightened. I used that time to build the agency's next program and the tools to run it. Everything in this section is work product for the agency, made with AI as the builder and me as the designer, tester, and user.

Screen Reader Foundations: a curriculum structured with AI

Screen Reader Foundations is the remote, guided self-study program I designed for Windows screen reader users. It grew out of the teaching plan I developed at Bosma. Most of the subject content is not mine: the program is built around a published textbook, the Windows Screen Reader Primer by David Kingsbury. What I add is years of class notes, the order I teach things in, and what I know trips people up. AI did the structuring that would have taken me months alone:

  • Turned my notes into a program document Vocational Rehabilitation could evaluate, with entry requirements, an intake assessment, session format, and outcomes.
  • Laid out four tracks against employment goals: Business Enterprise Program preparation, customer service, higher education, and general use.
  • Built a milestone workbook, chapter-by-chapter class notes, and quizzes for the chapters of the Windows Screen Reader Primer, including two classes on using AI itself.
  • Designed the blank templates for the student homework logs, the self-grading quizzes, the mini-projects, and the monthly progress reports that go to VR counselors.

This is what using AI at work looks like when the worker knows the subject. I did not ask it what to teach. The textbook and my own teaching experience settled that, and AI helped me organize, format, and check.

Classtronomical: an AI student in every class, running on my own laptop

Taking notes with a screen reader is a skill of its own. A student who is new to screen readers cannot be expected to use that skill while still learning everything else about the screen reader in the same class. So after each class, a pipeline I built does it for them, and the student wakes up to a study guide in their inbox.

  • Transcription happens on my computer, using an open speech-recognition model.
  • Note-taking is done by a small open language model, also on my computer. It reads the transcript against my lesson plan and writes about ten notes, each with a headline, the way an attentive student would.
  • Assembly and checking are done by ordinary code, not by the AI. Product names are spell-checked against my notes. A tip attributed to me is dropped unless those words are actually in the transcript. The list of keystrokes mentioned in class is found by pattern-matching, so it cannot be invented.
  • Delivery is an accessible email, sent overnight.

The production pipeline has been running this way since June 2026. Two things drove the design. Cost: there is no per-student or per-month fee, which matters to a small nonprofit. And privacy: a class recording contains a client's voice, questions, and sometimes personal details, and none of it is sent to an outside AI service. Student classes have only ever been processed locally. It runs on an ordinary laptop processor with no graphics card, which meant learning to give a small model one narrow job at a time, making every step restartable after a crash, and adding a watchdog for stalls. Those are the skills employers are asking for right now: choosing the right model for a task, running it privately, and building the checks that make its output trustworthy.

The laptop quote pipeline

Students in the program need a business-grade Windows laptop, and the agency needs a written quote for VR before buying one. Shopping sites are among the least accessible places on the web. A tool I built in September 2026 searches eBay through its official interface and gathers listings from Amazon Renewed, Dell Refurbished, and Back Market; keeps only machines that meet the program specification; compares each against the Computers for the Blind price; and writes a Word quote ready to send. We used it to buy our first training laptop and will use it for the others as they are needed. Sourcing computers was part of my job when I had sight. I now do it far faster without sight, because AI helped me build a pipeline to do the searching.

The monthly tech brief

Keeping up with assistive technology news means checking dozens of sources, most of them cluttered. I have started using a research pipeline that gathers recent developments for blind and low-vision users in the United States, merges duplicate coverage, separates a vendor's claim from verified accessibility, and presents the results as one accessible briefing page where I mark each story include, hold, or skip. The newsletter is then written from my selections. Nothing is published automatically. It is new, begun in September 2026, and I am still tuning it.

What all of this rests on

I am not asking anyone to certify me to teach software development or artificial intelligence. I could build that curriculum, and I expect blind people will need it soon. What I am saying I can teach is the thing underneath all of it: using a screen reader well, efficiently, and independently. Every tool in this section was specified, tested, and put to work by someone who cannot see the screen. None of it happens without screen reader fundamentals, and screen reader fundamentals are exactly what Screen Reader Foundations teaches.

What else I built this year

These were built on my own time, the same way: AI doing the typing and me doing the thinking, specifying, testing, and deciding. I am the project manager, the accessibility tester, and the only user who matters for the first version. None of it was possible for me before 2026.

NVDA add-ons I built

An NVDA add-on is a Python program that runs inside the screen reader itself. These were written for NVDA 2026.1 and packaged with the standard add-on build tools. The two I use most come first.

  • Favorites Hub. One place for the folders, links, text snippets, command-line commands, and keystroke macros I use constantly, reached by a fuzzy quick-pick so I type three letters and press Enter. It can capture the folder I am standing in, the path in an open Save dialog, or the address in my browser without my retyping it.
  • Speech Harvester. Keeps a memory-only history of everything NVDA has spoken, lets me scrub back through it by keyboard, and pulls structured data out of it by pattern: a phone number, an email address, a list of items read off one screen that I need to enter into another. It is how I move data between two systems that were never meant to talk to each other.
  • File Collector. Gathers scattered files for an AI to review. Paste a messy list of file names, and it finds them in the open project or Explorer window and copies them into a staging folder, renaming collisions, zipping the result with a manifest of original paths, and refusing payloads big enough to crash a browser. As I browse files it plays a short beep when I land on one I was looking for, so I can collect by ear.
  • Pivot. A workspace launcher. I describe a working layout once, say the two AI desktop apps, a project folder, a terminal, and a browser on a given page, and Pivot opens all of it in order, puts each window where it belongs, sets focus, and reuses anything already running.
  • Overhead Inspector. An experiment: a performance profiler that reports screen reader slowdowns as sound instead of a graph.

Snappy Play, a Windows audio player

A keyboard-only audio player with no visual interface at all: every function is a key press, and all feedback is spoken through NVDA. I built it because VLC felt slow with a screen reader and often failed to take focus. It starts playing within about a fifth of a second of opening a file. It was planned, built in Python with Claude Code, and tested with NVDA in a couple of hours on the morning of November 6, 2026. The full write-up is on the features page.

For blind users, live on the web

  • Accessible Bible (live). An offline-first, keyboard-driven Bible study tool for screen reader users, with continuous audio playback, commentary overlays, and an AI study-plan generator. Launched in early 2026.
  • Not By Sight: Blindness in the Bible (live). A full study site with recorded episodes. Every quotation is checked by automated tests against the published source text on every build, and the site refuses to publish if a single word drifts. Source code is public.
  • Keyboard Commander (live). Audio-first typing instruction for screen reader users. Built because 30 words per minute is the entry requirement for my own training program and most new students do not have it.
  • YAY! Accessible Dice (live). A Yahtzee-style game designed with zero standard form inputs, one focus trap, and a spoken announcement for every state change. Also a teaching example of how to build for screen readers.

  • Accessible Football (in development). A coaching career simulator for keyboard-only players, with a seeded game engine that runs the same in a browser and in automated tests.

Experiments and projects for other people

  • Reading Symbolic Universal Notation by speech, an experiment. Symbolic Universal Notation, or SUN, is a symbol-based Scripture system for Deaf and DeafBlind readers, published by Wycliffe Associates. I did not do this for them and have never worked for them. In October 2026 I was experimenting with Claude and wanted to know whether it could help me build a dictionary for NVDA that would let a screen reader speak symbols that are meant to be seen. Working only from the published SUN New Testament, we found that its 1,306 symbols were ordinary characters in a custom font, matched all 7,955 verses against the English they were made from, and produced a gloss for 89 percent of the words plus an NVDA pronunciation dictionary. With it, NVDA reads a page of SUN aloud as words.
  • Be The Way podcast promo pipeline. Drop an episode in a folder; three promo clips, branded audio and video, and a written rationale arrive by email half an hour later. Local transcription, about ten cents of AI per episode.
  • Wagglesworth Estate. A public story website for a friend's community, built so a non-developer can maintain it through a desktop chat assistant after handoff.
  • Helen Keller study. Fourteen research packets so far, from verified primary sources, toward a site on how Keller demonstrated the capacities of blind people to a sighted public. That is also the point of this page.

On certification, honestly

Indiana VR has told First Aid for the Blind, the agency I work for, that its credentials on file do not meet the minimum for a registered assistive technology training provider. The credential that field usually means is the CATIS from ACVREP. I want to say plainly what I think about that, because I respect the people who hold it.

CATIS is a good credential. It certifies that a professional can assess a client, teach assistive technology, and keep up with the field. It was designed, like most certifications in blindness rehabilitation, for a sighted professional entering the field, with a degree, supervised hours, and an exam as the way to prove competence they would otherwise have no way to show.

I have a different way to show it. I have used a screen reader for every working hour for more than eleven years. I managed a company's technology for seventeen years, so I know what a computer is doing underneath the screen reader. I have taught more than 155 people one-on-one, 65 of them on a computer screen reader, and worked with more than 200 in all, and I wrote the program VR is being asked to fund. And since 2025 I have been building accessible software, including the screen reader add-ons I work with every day, which no certification covers because the tools are less than two years old.

The certifications I do hold say something about how I think about them. I have held the JAWS Certification since 2019, my first year at Bosma, because that is the credential agencies ask for, and passed the current exam in January 2026 with a score of 89 percent. I use NVDA by choice and would sit its certification on request. I earned the Salesforce Certified Administrator credential after a four-month program, on a platform that is largely inaccessible to screen readers, in 2018. My instructors, who were working with the accessibility team at Salesforce, told me I was among the first blind people to earn it; I pass that on as what I was told, not as something I can prove. I kept it current through 2023, then let it go, because I knew I would never build a career on that platform and had decided to spend my working life on blind people instead. A certification is worth keeping when it serves the work. It is not the work.

A certification board asks for continuing education as proof that someone is keeping up. My proof is Screen Reader Foundations itself. Writing it meant working through the current versions of JAWS, NVDA, Windows, and Office, and the AI tools that arrived while I was writing, and deciding what a new screen reader user needs from each. The program is the record of that study.

The question for a training provider is whether the student ends up independent. My record says yes. I would welcome any reasonable path to equivalency, and I would welcome a conversation with anyone at VR who wants to see this work firsthand.

Where this is going

I think the next five years change what "assistive technology training" means. Three things I am sure of:

  • Blind people must be taught to use AI, now. It is a teacher, a sighted assistant, a memory, and an agent that can do inaccessible tasks on our behalf. A training program that teaches JAWS keystrokes and not this is already behind. I am building AI into Screen Reader Foundations as a core skill, not an elective.
  • Desktop AI applications are the new accessibility layer. When an app or website is inaccessible, an agent can operate it for me. That does not excuse bad design, but it ends the era where one unlabeled button could end a workday. The skill to teach is how to direct the agent well and check its work.
  • Blind people can now build their own tools. Every site and add-on on this page exists because a blind user could finally say what he needed and have it built. The things we build for ourselves are better for us than things built about us. I want to train the next blind instructors to do the same.

There is a number behind this. In 2024, 52.9 percent of working-age Americans with vision difficulty were employed, up from 46.1 percent in 2019, and about half of that gain is people working from home. Yet workers with vision difficulty still hold remote jobs at a lower rate than other workers. A remote job is done entirely through a computer, which makes screen reader fluency the job skill. I tabulated those figures from Census data myself, with AI, after an AI model gave me a number I did not trust: Blind employment and remote work: what the numbers say.

None of this has a certification yet. It is too new. Somebody has to be doing the work before anyone can write the exam, and I would rather be doing the work.

How this page was made

When I started this, I did not pull out an old resume and bring it up to date. It has been a very long time since I finished one. I started one just before Bosma hired me, and they never needed to see it. I had not started one when Kurt called with the offer from First Aid for the Blind. So I started from scratch.

I sat down and talked through my working life into a recorder for about an hour, from mowing yards at thirteen to this year. I transcribed the recording on my own machine, handed the transcript to Claude, and gave it the task. While it read the transcript it was also going through my local project folders, checking what I had said against the files, and looking up what the certification in question actually covers, all at the same time.

Then it turned editor. It built a timeline from what I had said, pointed out where my memory and my own files disagreed, and wrote me lists of questions about dates and details, which I answered in a text file. A digital recorder, transcription on my own computer, and the latest AI models are how I pieced together nearly forty years of dates.

The first version of this site took about twenty minutes to produce. I went through it with my screen reader, checked its work, had it make corrections, and kept adding to it over the next two days. In all it has taken about four hours of my own time.

That is the demonstration. A totally blind person with a screen reader, a recorder, and an AI, producing in an afternoon what would once have taken a sighted professional a week, or taken me not at all.
