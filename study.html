Blind employment and remote work: what the numbers say

An AI model told me that about 55 percent of blind people are now employed and that remote work is the biggest reason. I did not take its word for it. This page is what I found when I checked.

The short answer

  • The employment figure was close. In 2024, 52.9 percent of working-age Americans with vision difficulty were employed, up from 46.1 percent in 2019. That is a gain of 6.8 points in five years. Everyone else gained 1.3 points over the same period.
  • Remote work is a large part of the gain, and probably most of it. By simple arithmetic, work from home is about half of the rise. When I hold the mix of people fixed, so that a change in who is counted does not blur the picture, it is about 69 percent, with a wide margin of error. "The biggest reason" was closer to right than my first pass suggested, though this data cannot prove cause.
  • The surprise. Workers with vision difficulty are less likely to work from home than other workers, not more: 10.6 percent against 13.3 percent in 2024. Remote work opened a door, and blind and low-vision workers have gone through it at a lower rate than everyone else.
  • Education is the dividing line. Among workers with vision difficulty in 2024, 17.4 percent of those with a bachelor's degree worked from home, against 8.3 percent of those without. Among people with no degree, workers with vision difficulty worked from home in 2024 at almost the same rate as everyone else.
  • Many people who already have a degree are not working. About 826,000 working-age people with vision difficulty hold a bachelor's degree. 72.8 percent of them are employed, against 85.9 percent of other degree holders. About 224,000 are not working.
  • Not all of the employment gain is new jobs. Between 2.0 and 2.8 of the 6.8 points come from a change in who reports vision difficulty. The rest are gains within groups of similar people.
  • "70 percent unemployed" does not describe this group. In 2024, 47.1 percent were not employed, and most of those were not looking for work. The unemployment rate was 8.4 percent. See the 70 percent figure.
  • These figures have been checked. They match the Census Bureau's own published numbers, and a second program written independently reproduced them exactly. See how this was checked.

All figures are for ages 18 to 64 and come from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. "Vision difficulty" is the survey's term, and it is broader than blindness. I explain that under what these numbers cannot show.

The margin of error on the vision-difficulty figures is about plus or minus 0.6 points in each year, so the rise from 2019 to 2024 is far larger than sampling error. There is no standard file for 2020, because the pandemic disrupted that year's survey.

In 2024 about 2.33 million working-age people with vision difficulty were employed, out of 4.39 million. The unemployment rate, meaning people without work who are looking for it, was 8.4 percent. Another 42.2 percent were not working and not looking. The often-repeated claim that 70 percent of blind people are unemployed confuses those two groups.

The 70 percent figure

It is often said that 70 percent of blind people are unemployed. Here is what this data says about working-age people with vision difficulty in 2024.

  • Not employed: 47.1 percent, plus or minus 0.6. In 2019 it was 53.9 percent.
  • Unemployed, in the official sense of being without work and looking for it: 8.4 percent of those in the labor force.
  • Not working and not looking: 42.2 percent of the whole group. This is the large number, and it is the one that deserves attention.

So "70 percent unemployed" is wrong twice for this group. The share not working is 47 percent, not 70. And most of those people are not unemployed in the sense the word has for everyone else. The National Research and Training Center on Blindness and Low Vision makes the same point from the same survey.

That does not make the old figure wrong for everyone, and it would be dishonest to say so.

  • Vision difficulty together with another disability: 65.8 percent are not employed. That is about 2.23 million people, half of the group.
  • The same people, without a bachelor's degree: 69.0 percent are not employed.
  • Vision difficulty and no other disability: 27.9 percent are not employed. For people with no vision difficulty the figure is 23.6 percent.

This survey also cannot separate people who are totally blind from people with low vision, and most people who report vision difficulty have some usable vision. Employment among people who are totally blind is very likely lower than the figures on this page. I do not have data that says how much lower. Anyone who gives a figure for totally blind adults, whether 70 percent or 30, should be asked where it came from. A different federal survey asks how much difficulty a person has seeing, and it is the next thing to study.

Work from home among workers with vision difficulty went from 4.6 percent in 2019 to a peak of 13.9 percent in 2021, and has settled at 10.6 percent. The number of people more than tripled, from about 78,000 to about 240,000.

In every year, before the pandemic and after, workers with vision difficulty were less likely to work from home than other workers. The survey asks how a person usually got to work in the previous week, so someone who works from home one or two days a week is counted as a commuter. Hybrid work is undercounted for both groups.

How much of the gain is work from home

To see what remote work contributed, I split everyone at work into two groups, at home and on site, and measured each as a share of the whole working-age population.

Percent of all people ages 18 to 64 who were at work, by where they worked, 2019 and 2024
Group and place of work20192024Change
Vision difficulty, working from home2.1 percent5.5 percentup 3.4 points
Vision difficulty, working on site42.6 percent45.9 percentup 3.3 points
No vision difficulty, working from home4.0 percent10.0 percentup 6.0 points
No vision difficulty, working on site69.6 percent65.0 percentdown 4.6 points

For people with vision difficulty, the share at work rose 6.7 points. About 51 percent of that rise is people working from home, and the rest is people working on site. Both went up.

That split is simple arithmetic on the whole group, and the whole group changed. As the part on who is being counted shows, more of the people now reporting vision difficulty have no other disability, and most of that group works on site. Their arrival adds on-site workers to the count without anyone changing jobs.

When I hold the mix of those two groups fixed, work from home is about 69 percent of the rise, plus or minus 12. With a finer division, by age and education as well, it is about 80 percent, plus or minus 17. The margins are wide, but both figures are above half. The simple figure of 51 percent has a margin of plus or minus 7.

Counted in people instead of rates, about 29 percent of the added workers work from home. That figure is lower because the group itself grew 16 percent, and a larger group adds workers of every kind.

For everyone else the pattern is different. Work from home rose by 6.0 points while on-site work fell by 4.6. For most workers, remote work replaced a commute. For workers with vision difficulty, it added jobs, and on-site work did not shrink.

This is arithmetic, not proof of cause. Some of the people now working from home would have found on-site work if remote work did not exist, and some on-site jobs exist because a hybrid arrangement made them possible.

Who works from home, and who does not

The first results raised more questions, so I went back to the same Census files and asked them.

A degree matters more than vision does. A person with vision difficulty and a bachelor's degree is employed at 72.8 percent, slightly above a person with no vision difficulty and no degree. Among people without a degree, the work-from-home rates for the two groups are 8.3 and 8.7 percent. The difference of 0.4 points is inside its margin of error of 0.5, so in 2024 there is no real difference. In 2022 and 2023 workers with vision difficulty were a little behind (8.1 against 9.6 percent in 2022), so this is a gap that has closed.

A large part of the overall gap in remote work comes from this: 25.9 percent of employed people with vision difficulty hold a bachelor's degree, against 40.1 percent of other workers. If workers with vision difficulty had degrees at the same rate as everyone else, about 11.9 percent of them would work from home. The actual figure is 10.6 and other workers are at 13.3. So education accounts for about half of the gap. Done the other way round, giving other workers the education mix of workers with vision difficulty, it accounts for about 62 percent.

People who already have a degree

The table above could be read as an argument for reaching blind children early so that they finish school. That matters, but it is not the main thing these numbers describe. In 2024, 73 percent of working-age people with vision difficulty were 35 or older, and 46 percent were 50 to 64. Many of them finished their education and built a career before their vision changed. The survey does not ask when a person's vision loss began, so it cannot count them separately.

What it can show is the people who already hold a degree. About 826,000 working-age people with vision difficulty have a bachelor's degree or higher. 72.8 percent are employed, against 85.9 percent of degree holders without vision difficulty. About 224,000 are not working, and about 194,000 of those are not looking for work.

The gap is widest from 50 to 64, the ages at which sight loss from conditions such as diabetes and glaucoma becomes more common. These are people whose education is already paid for and whose experience is already earned.

Two cautions. Degree holders who report vision difficulty and no other disability are employed at 82.7 percent. That is 3.2 points below other degree holders, a much smaller gap, and most of them have usable vision. Those who report another disability as well are at 55.0 percent. And this data cannot say how many of the people not working would return to work with screen reader training.

Work experience

A degree is one kind of asset. Work experience is another, and the survey records when a person last worked. In 2024, about 2.07 million working-age people with vision difficulty were not employed. About 731,000 of them, 35.3 percent, had worked within the past five years. 12.6 percent had worked within the past year.

  • With a bachelor's degree and not working: 49.5 percent, about 111,000 people, worked within the past five years.
  • Without a degree and not working: 33.6 percent, about 620,000 people.
  • For comparison: among people with no vision difficulty who are not employed, 50.2 percent worked within the past five years.

These are people with a recent job behind them. The survey does not say why they stopped, or whether vision loss was the reason.

Workers with vision difficulty are less often in management and professional work, where remote jobs are concentrated, and more often in service, production, and transport work, which can rarely be done from home. If they held the same mix of jobs as everyone else, about 11.5 percent would work from home. Occupation accounts for about a third of the gap, or about 45 percent done the other way round. Education and occupation overlap, because a degree leads to a profession, so the two figures should not be added. Taken together they account for between 55 and 69 percent of the gap. This agrees with the conclusion of the earlier research: the kinds of jobs people hold limit how far remote work can reach.

One row stands out. In office and administrative support, which includes customer service, 14.4 percent of workers with vision difficulty work from home, close to the 15.7 percent for other workers. That is remote work that does not require a degree.

Who is being counted

The number of people reporting vision difficulty and no other disability grew 29 percent between 2019 and 2024. The number reporting vision difficulty together with another disability grew 5 percent. These two groups are in very different positions: in 2024, 72.1 percent of the first group were employed and 34.2 percent of the second.

Because the group with higher employment grew faster, the overall rate would have risen even if nobody new had found work. Holding the mix as it was in 2019, the employment rate in 2024 would be 51.0 percent, not 52.9. So of the 6.8-point gain, about 4.9 points are gains within each group and about 1.9 points are a change in who is counted. A finer division, by age and education as well as other disability, puts the change in who is counted at about 2.8 points and the gains within groups at about 4.1. Employment did rise in both groups, by 4.1 and 5.5 points.

Pay and kind of job

  • People who work from home earn more. In 2024, median earnings over the previous twelve months for workers with vision difficulty were $50,000 (plus or minus $4,383) for those working from home and $38,000 (plus or minus $1,207) for those on site. For other workers the figures were $73,800 and $50,000. Much of that difference reflects education, since remote workers more often have degrees.
  • Remote work has become ordinary employment. In 2019, 35 percent of people with vision difficulty who worked from home were self-employed. In 2024 it was 17 percent. Remote work has shifted from self-employment toward regular jobs.
  • Most of it is full time. 80 percent of those working from home in 2024 usually worked 35 hours or more a week.

Indiana

About 105,000 working-age people in Indiana reported vision difficulty in 2024. One state is a small sample, so I pooled 2022 through 2024, and the margins of error are wide.

  • Employment: 51.4 percent, plus or minus 2.1, in 2022 through 2024. In 2019 it was 46.7 percent, plus or minus 3.4. For other Indiana residents it is 77.3 percent.
  • Work from home: 8.4 percent of Indiana workers with vision difficulty, plus or minus 1.6, against 10.2 percent of other Indiana workers. Both are below the national figures.

Indiana follows the national pattern: employment is up, and remote work is reaching workers with vision difficulty somewhat less than others. Both differences only just clear their margins of error, so read them as likely, not certain. The Census Bureau's own published employment figure for Indiana over the same years is 52.0 percent.

What published research says

  • My 2023 figures match the field's own. The National Research and Training Center on Blindness and Low Vision at Mississippi State University reports 52.3 percent employed, 8.1 percent unemployed, and 43.1 percent not in the labor force for 2023, from the same survey. My tabulation gives 52.2, 8.0, and 43.3. The small differences are expected, because the public file is a sample of the records the Census Bureau uses for its own estimates.
  • People who lose vision in the middle of a career often leave work, and that is changing. McDonnall and Cmar, in the journal WORK in 2024, used a national study that interviews the same people every two years. They found 167 workers, ages 44 to 64, who developed vision loss while employed, and compared them with 800 similar workers who did not. About 60 percent were still working when they first reported the vision loss, against 87 percent of the comparison group. Two years later it was 49 percent against 80. Those who retired were much more likely to say they were forced to, and to be dissatisfied with retirement. The share who kept working rose steadily over the years and was highest from 2014 to 2018. The authors connect that to advances in assistive technology, including screen readers built into every major computer and phone.
  • Health, more than vision, predicts who keeps a job. Cmar, McDonnall, and Mitchell, in the Journal of Aging and Health in 2025, studied 248 people from the same national study who developed a visual impairment at ages 44 to 64 while working. About 68 percent were working when they first reported it. Poor health and a larger number of chronic conditions made it less likely; being married or partnered made it more likely. Education level made no difference to keeping a job. That agrees with what this page finds about other disabilities, and it means the asset at stake is the job and the experience a person already has.
  • Degree holders who are working are more often in jobs below their education. McDonnall and Cmar, in the Journal of Visual Impairment and Blindness in 2024, used the 2019 file of the same Census survey I used. Among working people with an associate degree or higher, 62.4 percent of those with a visual impairment held a job that typically requires less education than they have, against 55.7 percent of others.
  • About one in five receives Social Security, and few of them work. McDonnall, Cmar, and McKnight, in the same journal in 2022, found that 19.8 percent of working-age people with a visual impairment received Social Security income. Of those, 12.4 percent had worked in the past year, against 58.8 percent of people not receiving it. Earlier studies they cite found that people limit their earnings to keep benefits and health coverage. This is part of why so many are not looking for work.
  • Researchers had already seen the same gap before the pandemic. Schur, Ameri, and Kruse, in the Journal of Occupational Rehabilitation in 2020, used the same Census survey for 2009 to 2018 and found that, once education and occupation are taken into account, workers with vision or hearing impairments were less likely than workers without disabilities to work primarily at home. My figures show the same gap and carry it forward through 2024.
  • Telework mattered most for people with vision impairments. The same research group, in a 2022 paper by Ameri, Kruse, Park, Rodgers, and Schur, found that when labor markets tightened during the pandemic, about 71 percent of the resulting rise in employment among people with vision impairments was telework, a larger share than for most other disability groups. They also conclude that the kinds of jobs disabled workers hold limit how far telework can reach. That is a different survey and a different method from mine, and it points the same way: remote work is a real part of the gain, and it has not reached blind workers as fully as it could.
  • Jobs that can be done remotely drove the recovery for people with disabilities generally. Ne'eman and Maestas, in the Disability and Health Journal in 2023, found that employment gains for people with disabilities after the pandemic were concentrated in occupations that can be done by telework. Their study covers all disabilities, not vision alone, and they caution that some of the gain may reflect more people becoming disabled.
  • Disabled workers say remote work matters to them. A study of 1,221 disabled remote and hybrid workers in the United Kingdom, led by Lancaster University, found that 85 percent called remote or hybrid work essential or very important when looking for a new job. That is a survey of people already working remotely, in another country, so it shows preference and not cause.
  • A different federal survey gives lower employment numbers. The American Foundation for the Blind's published figures from the Current Population Survey showed about 35 percent of people ages 16 to 64 with vision difficulty employed in 2017. The two surveys reach people differently and are not comparable. Anyone quoting a number should say which survey it came from.

What these numbers cannot show

  • "Vision difficulty" is not "blind." The survey asks whether a person is "blind or has serious difficulty seeing even when wearing glasses." Most people who answer yes have some usable vision. Nothing in this data separates screen reader users from people who use magnification or nothing at all. Employment among totally blind people is very likely lower than these figures.
  • The group itself grew. The number of working-age people reporting vision difficulty rose 16 percent, from 3.79 million to 4.39 million. I measured part of this above: between 2.0 and 2.8 of the 6.8 points gained reflect a shift toward people who report vision difficulty alone. Other changes in who answers yes to the question cannot be seen in this data.
  • Cause is not established. The data shows that employment rose and that work from home rose. It does not show that one produced the other.
  • Hybrid work is mostly invisible here. Only people who usually worked from home are counted as working from home.

What it means for training

I went looking for a number to support my own work. The data gave me a more careful argument than the one I started with, and I think a better one.

Remote work is real for blind and low-vision workers, it pays better, and it is now ordinary employment. But it is reaching people with degrees and office jobs far more than anyone else. Screen reader training does not hand anyone a degree or a profession, and I will not claim it does.

It does not have to. Most of the adults I teach are not starting from nothing. They have a degree, or a trade, or twenty years in a job, and then they lose their sight. Everything they know is still there. What they have lost is the way to reach it: the screen, the mouse, the printed page. A screen reader gives that back. The training is small next to the education and experience it unlocks, and it is the one missing piece.

I am one of those people. I had seventeen years as an information technology manager when I went blind in 2015. None of that knowledge left me. What I needed was a way to use a computer by ear, and once I had it, everything I knew was usable again. By 2019 I was teaching other people to do the same.

Researchers have followed people in this position, and what they found is under what published research says. Of workers who lost vision between ages 44 and 64, about half were no longer working two years later, and most of those who retired said they were forced to. The share who kept their jobs has risen over the years, and the researchers connect that to assistive technology, screen readers included. They conclude that many who stopped working could likely have continued with the right skills and accommodations, and that too few people know free services exist.

The numbers above say there are many more. About 224,000 working-age people with vision difficulty hold a bachelor's degree and are not working. About 731,000 who are not working, with or without a degree, held a job within the past five years. Some of them have other disabilities, and many have enough vision to work without a screen reader. But among them are the people for whom screen reader training is the shortest path back to a job, and the jobs they are qualified for are the ones that have moved online.

For people without a degree or an office background, screen reader fluency still comes first, but it is not the whole answer, and the training has to lead somewhere. That is why Screen Reader Foundations is organized around employment tracks, and two of them, higher education and customer service, lead to the work where the data shows remote jobs are.

A remote job is done entirely through a computer: there is no coworker at the next desk to read a screen aloud or click the button the screen reader cannot find. For a blind worker, the screen reader is the whole job. That is why the program is taught remotely on purpose. A student who completes it has spent months doing what a remote job requires: working alone at their own computer, in their own home, solving problems through a screen reader with help available only by phone or video call. The training is a rehearsal for the job.

How this was checked

Numbers about blind employment get argued over, so I tried to break these before anyone else does. One of the checks changed a conclusion, and several changed my wording.

Against the Census Bureau's own published figures

The Bureau publishes employment by type of disability for the same ages, in its table B18120, computed from its full set of records. The public file I used is a sample of those records, so small differences are expected.

The largest difference is 0.24 of a point, and each one is inside this study's margin of error of about 0.6 points. The population counts agree to within about 1 percent.

Against sample counts in published research

Two papers from the National Research and Training Center on Blindness and Low Vision used the 2019 file of the same survey and reported exactly how many survey records were in their samples. I drew the same samples with my own program. All 9 of the 9 figures I compared matched exactly, including record counts of 39,934 and 695,899 and the weighted totals of 4,093,503 and 69,925,153 people. That shows this study reads the file the way researchers in the field do.

Done a second time, independently

A second AI agent was given the Census files and a description, in words, of what to count. It was not allowed to see my program or my results, and it worked out the survey's variables and codes from the Bureau's documentation. It produced 40 figures for 2019 and 2024: population, employment, work from home, education, age, other disabilities, Indiana, earnings, and self-employment. All 40 matched mine exactly.

Read line by line

A third agent reviewed the programs against the Bureau's documentation for each of the five years, looking for a variable whose meaning changed between years, a wrong code, or a mistake in the arithmetic. It found no error that changes a number. It did find places where my wording claimed more than the numbers support, and rounding that did not add up. These are corrected:

  • I had written that people without a degree work from home at the same rate whether or not they have vision difficulty. That is true for 2024. In 2022 and 2023 there was a small gap.
  • I had written that degree holders with vision difficulty alone are employed at a rate "close to everyone else." They are 3.2 points below, which is more than the margin of error.
  • I had written that service, production, and transport work "cannot" be done from home. A small share of those workers do work from home.
  • The two Indiana comparisons only just clear their margins of error, and the page now says so.

Each estimate worked out a second way

  • How much of the rise is work from home. Simple arithmetic gives 51 percent, plus or minus 7. Holding the mix of people fixed gives 69 percent, plus or minus 12. This is the check that changed a conclusion: remote work is probably more than half of the real gain.
  • How much of the rise is a change in who is counted. Between 2.0 and 2.8 points, depending on how finely people are grouped. Which year's mix is held fixed makes almost no difference.
  • How much of the remote-work gap education and occupation explain. Education: 49 to 62 percent. Occupation: 33 to 45 percent. Both together: 55 to 69 percent. The range comes from which group's rates are applied to which group's mix.

Margins of error

Every difference this page relies on was tested against its 90 percent margin of error, worked out from the survey's 80 replicate weights.

  • Clear of the margin: the rise in employment (6.9 points before rounding, plus or minus 0.8); the remote-work gap in every year, from 0.9 points in 2019 (plus or minus 0.3) to 2.7 in 2024 (plus or minus 0.4); and the employment gap for degree holders at every age.
  • Not clear of the margin: the remote-work gap among people without a degree in 2024 (0.4 points, plus or minus 0.5), and the gap in office and administrative work (1.4 points, plus or minus 1.5). The page describes both as about the same, which is what those numbers support.

Where a figure covers more than one year, the years are treated as separate samples. That is a reasonable approximation and not the Census Bureau's official method for combining years. It affects only the Indiana figures.

What has not been done

No researcher in the field has reviewed this yet. That is the next step. All of the checks above were run by AI agents under my direction, and the programs and their output are published below so that a person can repeat them.

How this was done

This page is also a demonstration of how I use AI for research, so here is exactly what happened.

  1. An AI model gave me the 55 percent figure and the remote-work explanation in conversation, with no source.
  2. I asked Claude to treat both as claims to be tested. It found the published figure, 52.3 percent, and read the original document to confirm it.
  3. At first I found no published work-from-home rates for people with vision difficulty. So Claude wrote a short program to compute them from the Census Bureau's public survey records, about 3 million records a year for five years.
  4. Before trusting the program, we checked it against the one number we could verify. Its 2023 employment figure came out at 52.2 percent against the published 52.3.
  5. The result contradicted what I had been told and what I hoped to find. It is reported here as it came out.
  6. I then asked whether anyone had done this before. They had: a research group at Rutgers University studied the question through 2020, and their findings agree with mine. I am not the first to look. As far as I can find, the figures for 2021 through 2024 have not been published elsewhere, and I would be glad to be corrected.
  7. The first results raised questions, so I asked them of the same files: education, occupation, who is being counted, pay, and Indiana. Two of the answers weakened the simple story I had hoped to tell. They are on this page.
  8. Then I tried to break it, in the four ways described under how this was checked. The numbers held. One conclusion moved: remote work turned out to be a larger share of the gain than my first arithmetic showed.
  9. Last, I read four papers from the research center whose figure I had started with. They had already studied the people this page is about, with data that follows the same people over time, which the Census survey cannot do. Their findings are now part of this page.
  10. One figure I had been given, about blind workers in a United Kingdom study, could not be confirmed at its source. It is not on this page.

My part was the questions, the judgment about what to trust, and reading every line of this page with a screen reader. The tables are marked up so that a screen reader announces the row and column for each number. The first version of the study took a few hours, and the checks were added the same day.

The lesson I would teach from this: an AI model will give you a confident number. A skilled user asks where it came from, and has the AI go and check.

The follow-up tables come from two more programs, tabulate_detail.py and analyse_cube.py, with their combined output in followup.json. Occupation groups follow the Census Bureau's 2018 occupation codes. The "what if" figures for education and occupation apply the work-from-home rates of workers with vision difficulty to the education or occupation mix of other workers.

The checks come from check_work.py and tabulate_extra.py, with their output in checks.json. The comparison with published sample counts is check_published.py, with output in published_check.json. The independent program is independent_replicate.py and its output is independent.json.

To repeat the tabulation: the program is tabulate_acs.py, and its output for each year is in the same folder as a small data file per year. Download a year's person file from the Census Bureau, run the program on it, and compare.

Method in brief: ages 18 to 64, civilian, not living in an institution. Employed means at work or with a job but temporarily absent. Work-from-home figures are for people at work in the survey's reference week. Margins of error are at 90 percent confidence, from the survey's 80 replicate weights.
